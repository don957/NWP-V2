Name: Kelly Morris
Badge: Joining a Conversation
What is one insight you gained from completing the Atwoodian Table yourself that might shape how you introduce it to students?:

One insight I gained from completing the Atwoodian Table myself is how important it is to slow down and intentionally summarize each text before forming an opinion. Working through the table required me to carefully identify the author’s main point, perspective, and supporting ideas rather than quickly reacting to the topic. Because of this experience, I plan to introduce the activity by modeling how to summarize a text and identify the author’s viewpoint so students can clearly understand what each source is saying. This will help students focus on analyzing multiple perspectives first, which will allow them to develop more thoughtful and informed responses when they share their own ideas.

How does the Atwoodian Table idea compare to how you have taught argument and/or responding to multiple texts in the past?:
The Atwoodian Table approach is different from how I have typically taught argument or responses to multiple texts in the past. Previously, I often used verbal discussion questions or had students analyze a single quote, speech, or text and explain what they believed it meant. While those activities encouraged participation and discussion, students were often working from only one perspective. The Atwoodian Table strengthens this process by requiring students to examine several viewpoints before forming their own opinion. By reading or listening to multiple sources with differing perspectives, students must summarize each text and analyze how the ideas compare or conflict. This approach encourages deeper critical thinking and helps students move beyond simply repeating what they already believe, allowing them to develop more informed and thoughtful responses based on a range of information.
What do you notice about the two sets of excerpts in this module (children in fancy restaurants and toddlers and technology)? How do the excerpts work together to invite students to join the conversation? What are some conversations related to topics you teach?:
One thing I noticed about the two sets of excerpts in this module—children in fancy restaurants and toddlers and technology—is that they present a range of perspectives on topics that many students can easily relate to. Each excerpt offers a slightly different opinion, experience, or argument, which allows the texts to function like a conversation. Instead of presenting one clear answer, the readings give students multiple viewpoints to consider. As students work through the excerpts, they are able to see how different authors support their ideas and how those ideas may agree, disagree, or build on one another. This structure naturally invites students to join the conversation because they are exposed to several opinions before they develop and share their own thoughts. Using this type of text set encourages students to summarize each author’s main idea, analyze the reasoning behind the argument, and then reflect on where they stand on the issue. This is especially valuable because secondary students often have strong opinions, but those opinions are sometimes influenced primarily by peers or people in their lives rather than by careful analysis of information. By examining multiple perspectives first, students are better prepared to form thoughtful responses and recognize that their voice matters in an academic discussion. In the subjects I teach, similar conversations could be created around historical and civic topics. For example, students could analyze multiple perspectives on whether the government should expand or limit its power, how different groups viewed the American Revolution, or how citizens responded to major events such as the Great Depression. Other conversations could focus on topics like federalism, individual rights versus public safety, or differing viewpoints during major reform movements. Presenting students with multiple historical voices and interpretations would allow them to analyze the evidence, understand how perspectives differ, and then contribute their own informed ideas to the discussion.
