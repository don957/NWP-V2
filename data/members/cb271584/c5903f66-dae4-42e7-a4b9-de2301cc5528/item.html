Name: Adam Rich
Badge: Joining a Conversation
What is one insight you gained from completing the Atwoodian Table yourself that might shape how you introduce it to students?:

The important thing for students to understand about argumentative writing is that is on-going and evolving, just like a face-to-face conversation. This strategy is simple, yet creates an effective way to frame research as something more than just gathering facts. The Atwoodian Table promotes the evaluation of claims by inviting students to join the argument with their own thinking. I believe students want to do this kind of work because it is so naturally engaging.

There are many topics in social studies that I could use with this strategy. My biggest challenge is finding texts that have a clear claim contained within a short excerpt AND are written at an appropriate reading level for middle school students. So much of what is provided to me is encyclopedic in nature. Claims developed by academic historians are developed over many pages of dense writing. Primary sources are equally complex.

How does the Atwoodian Table idea compare to how you have taught argument and/or responding to multiple texts in the past?:
In the past, I fear that my notetaking process siloed author's arguments. This format promotes compare/contrast evaluation of multiple sources.
What do you notice about the two sets of excerpts in this module (children in fancy restaurants and toddlers and technology)? How do the excerpts work together to invite students to join the conversation? What are some conversations related to topics you teach?:
The excerpts were centered on a focused topic. They didn't necessarily speak to each other directly, but each author had a clear claim and reasons to support it. Additionally, they each used different modes of persuasion to connect with their audience. This opens up another level of evaluation and discussion. Social studies addresses many of the big ideas mention in this lesson, such as, security vs. liberty. I can this being used to evaluate the decisions or actions of historical figures, or to wrestle with competing claims to land.
