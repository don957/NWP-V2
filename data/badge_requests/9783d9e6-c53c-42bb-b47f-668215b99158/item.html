Which strategy from this module (Daily Schedule, Take a Line for a Walk or Interpreting Visual Texts) feels most immediately usable in your teaching context? Why?:

The strategy that feels most immediately usable in my teaching context is Interpreting Visual Texts.

As a middle school science teacher, I already rely heavily on diagrams, models, and visuals—things like food webs, Punnett squares, rock cycle diagrams, and body system charts. This strategy fits naturally into what I’m already doing, but it pushes it a step further by being more intentional about how students interact with those visuals. Instead of just showing a diagram and explaining it, I can have students analyze it, ask questions, and make evidence-based observations.

What other strategies or approaches have you used to engage your students in routine writing over the course of this school year? What, if any, challenges have you encountered in engaging students in sustained routine writing?:
Over the course of this school year, I’ve used several strategies to build routine writing into my science classroom. One of the most consistent has been CER (Claim, Evidence, Reasoning) responses, especially after labs or real-world scenarios. I’ve also incorporated bell ringers/exit tickets that ask students to explain a concept in their own words, make a prediction, or reflect on what they learned. At times, I’ve used quick writes or science journals, where students respond to prompts like “What do you notice?” or “What does this data suggest?” to get them thinking without the pressure of a formal grade. These approaches have helped make writing a more normal part of our classroom routine, but there have definitely been challenges. One of the biggest is student stamina—many students are hesitant to write more than a few sentences and can become disengaged when asked to sustain their thinking over a longer response.
