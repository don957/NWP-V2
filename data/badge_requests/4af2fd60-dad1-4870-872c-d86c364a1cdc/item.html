Looking back on this inquiry cycle, what did you learn about your teaching and your students that you might not have noticed otherwise? How did engaging in this teacher-research process shape or shift your thinking about your classroom practice?:

Looking back on this inquiry cycle, I learned that my students are much more capable of engaging with complex texts than I initially thought, especially when the work is broken down into manageable steps. I noticed that when I provided structure and time for discussion, students were more willing to participate, ask questions, and support their ideas with evidence. One strategy that worked especially well was Say-Mean-Matter. This approach gave students a clear way to process the text and, more importantly, helped build their confidence in their writing because they felt like their thoughts and interpretations mattered.

This teacher-research process also helped me reflect on my own teaching practices. I realized that I sometimes move too quickly or assume students will automatically know how to approach challenging texts. By slowing down, chunking the material, and giving students consistent strategies like Say-Mean-Matter, I was able to keep them more engaged and on track. It shifted my thinking to focus more on scaffolding and student voice rather than just content coverage. Moving forward, I plan to continue using these strategies and be more intentional about creating space for discussion and reflection, as it clearly supports both understanding and confidence in my classroom

Think back to a moment during the lesson sequence that stands out. It could be a comment from a student, an unexpected challenge or success, a shift in engagement, or a moment when your own thinking changed. Describe what happened and why this moment feels meaningful to you now. What does it reveal:
One moment that really stood out was when students started using evidence in their writing to support their claims. At first, many of them were just sharing their opinions, but later I saw them going back into the text and pulling out examples to prove their point. This moment was important because it showed real growth. Students were able to back up what they were saying, which is something they had struggled with before. It showed me that the strategies we used were helping. This reveals that students can do this kind of thinking when they are given the right support and time. It also reminded me how important it is to teach students how to use evidence, not just expect them to do it

As I look over my students’ drafts, I am noticing some early patterns. Many students are starting to use evidence more, but some still struggle with fully explaining their reasoning. I also noticed that when students used structured strategies, their writing was more clear and organized. A surprise for me was how much their confidence improved, especially when they had a clear format to follow. I am most curious to explore how other students responded to this style of writing and if they showed similar growth. I would like to see how this approach works in different classrooms and with different types of learners. One question I have is how to continue developing this type of writing into a format that fits well in a History classroom. I want to better connect it to the kinds of readings and thinking skills that are important in specific history units, while still keeping the structure that helps students be successful.
