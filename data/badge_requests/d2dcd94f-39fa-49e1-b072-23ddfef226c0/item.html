Looking back on this inquiry cycle, what did you learn about your teaching and your students that you might not have noticed otherwise? How did engaging in this teacher-research process shape or shift your thinking about your classroom practice?:

I learned that starting a lesson with a question piques their interest much more than telling them a topic. The questions guide them to a topic, but in a more engaging way. I will forever start a lesson with a question rather than a statement about what I want them to learn. I will still do that, but not right away.

Think back to a moment during the lesson sequence that stands out. It could be a comment from a student, an unexpected challenge or success, a shift in engagement, or a moment when your own thinking changed. Describe what happened and why this moment feels meaningful to you now. What does it reveal:
During second quarter, I taught my kids how to write an argumentative essay. I have now learned I did it all wrong. I used too many rules and guides and structures to formulate an essay, but was I really teaching them how to argue? I don't think so. This strategy first teaches them how to think. Once they learn how to think about a topic, they can better choose one of their own based on questions they have first. I will definately engage my kids in this strategy before we write a final argumentative essay.

The early patterns I see are the interested minds when they have "stake in the game." Allowing them to first read or listen to multiple perspectives opens their minds to other views first. Before they were coming up with their own argument first then reading other opinions. This is reversed and it allows for them to dig deeper.
