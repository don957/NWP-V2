Badge: Joining a Conversation
What is one insight you gained from completing the Atwoodian Table yourself that might shape how you introduce it to students?:

One thing I noticed from doing the Atwoodian Table myself is that it really helps to look at different types of texts to understand a topic better. In an ag classroom, that’s important because a lot of topics—like farming practices or animal care—aren’t just one-sided. So when I introduce it, I’d want to use a mix of sources (like articles, videos, or data) and show students how each one gives a different perspective. I’d also remind them that the goal isn’t to find one right answer, but to understand the issue from multiple sides.

How does the Atwoodian Table idea compare to how you have taught argument and/or responding to multiple texts in the past?:
I haven’t really taught a traditional argument style before, so the Atwoodian Table feels a bit different from what I’ve done in the past. Usually, it’s more about students learning information and maybe sharing their opinion, but not really digging into multiple viewpoints in a structured way. What I like about the Atwoodian Table is that it naturally gets students to look at different ideas and respond to them. Instead of just saying what they think, they have to consider other perspectives, compare them, and build on them. It feels more like a real conversation than a formal argument essay.
What do you notice about the two sets of excerpts in this module (children in fancy restaurants and toddlers and technology)? How do the excerpts work together to invite students to join the conversation? What are some conversations related to topics you teach?:
One thing I notice about the two sets of excerpts is that they both show different opinions on everyday situations that people actually care about. They’re relatable, but also a little controversial, so it makes you want to pick a side or at least think more about it. Each set includes perspectives that kind of “push” against each other, which helps show that there isn’t just one right answer. They work together because they model what a real conversation looks like—different voices, different opinions, and some tension between them. That makes it easier for students to jump in, because they can agree, disagree, or add their own experiences. It feels more like joining a discussion than just answering questions. Conservations that I would have would be things like large scale farming/ small scale and differences between dairy and beef cattle.
