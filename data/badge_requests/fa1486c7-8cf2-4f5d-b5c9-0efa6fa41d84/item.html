What is one insight you gained from completing the Atwoodian Table yourself that might shape how you introduce it to students?:

​I am embarrassed to say that when I completed the Atwoodian Table, I got stuck on how to make the chairs around the table. I spent way too much time stressing out about it. I find this funny in retrospect because I can picture particular students that would struggle with the same thing. For this reason, I might provide a graphic organizer of a table with premade "chairs" the first time we complete this activity. Another problem, is I wrote so much in my claim and evidence for each "chair." I really struggle with summarizing. I always have. And here at 48, I am still struggling. I don't want to leave anything out that I might find important later because if it is not down, my brain just loses it.

How does the Atwoodian Table idea compare to how you have taught argument and/or responding to multiple texts in the past?:
If I am honest, I usually treat each text as its own individual thing and not as text set. I remember doing a pro/con list, but after I treated most of my texts independently of each other. I am realizing that the pro/con list or even compare/contrast was me throwing it in as an afterthought. I will definitely be more intentional in my scaffolding and will use some of these strategies so the students are working through the tests more intentionally so their learning is more cohesive.
What do you notice about the two sets of excerpts in this module (children in fancy restaurants and toddlers and technology)? How do the excerpts work together to invite students to join the conversation? What are some conversations related to topics you teach?:
​I like how the excerpts showcase different perspectives using first hand experience. We tend to draw our own conclusions without taking into account other perspectives. I know when I have done this in life, I am often wrong. For example, if I just casually heard that a restaurant banned kids without knowing why, I would probably unfairly judge the owners intentions. This activity showcases why it is important to learn other perspectives. In order to really frame your opinion, you need to view it from multiple perspectives. This allows students to see how real world problems (or even everyday) have many dimensions. Often times when you fix one problem, several more are uncovered. I am excited to use this in forensics next year during eyewitness testimony. I can provide excerpts of different perspectives on the reliability of eyewitness testimony. Currently, we reflect at several different points in the unit, however, I feel like this would be a good way to introduce the topic. My thought is to bring in some perspectives they will be learning about. Some perspectives I can think of off the top of my head are a victim of a crime, an investigator, an attorney, an exoneree from the innocence project that was wrongly convicted due to eyewitness testimony, a juror. This will also provide a perfect opportunity for reflection at the end of the unit. I can have them look back at their table to see how their thinking has changed even more once the unit is complete.
