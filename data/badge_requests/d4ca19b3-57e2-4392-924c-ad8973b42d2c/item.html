Name: Adam Rich
Badge: Reading to Write: They Say / I Say
Which version of the They Say / I Say routine (with visual texts or with annotation) feels like the best fit for your students right now? Why?:

Annotating texts is the best fit for They Say/I Say in my class at this time. We are in the middle of a unit on the American Revolution. There are some visual text, but they are not as accessible to middle school learners as the example in our presentation. The images are full of details that require some explanation. Although many of the text we will use also require explanation from the teacher, there are some text that could be broken into small excerpts that student can digest independently.

Describe one way you might adapt the They Say / I Say framework to align with the types of texts your students read most often (e.g., articles, visuals, multimodal texts).:

An upcoming lesson will explore the Declaration of Independence. I can see the They Say/I Say strategy being used to break down the preamble or isolate some of the key complaints listed in the document. Students could read and respond to the text on slips of paper like the presentation demonstrated. Beginning with some independent worktime add accountability for students to engage in the thinking, but I also feel that it's important to give them a chance to try out their thinking with a peer before they do any writing. I'd like for the students to finish by create their own summary of the text. This lesson allows students to build understanding and make connections rather than listening to me or watching a video.

What, if anything, do you need help thinking through as you plan for using They Say / I Say in your classroom?:

Is it ever okay to modify texts that students will engage with to remove barriers such as vocabulary?
