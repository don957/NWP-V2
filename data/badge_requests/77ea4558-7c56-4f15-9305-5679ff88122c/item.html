Looking back on this inquiry cycle, what did you learn about your teaching and your students that you might not have noticed otherwise? How did engaging in this teacher-research process shape or shift your thinking about your classroom practice?:

I noticed that students were more willing to engage when the writing felt manageable. Breaking the task into smaller layers probably lowered their resistance and helped build their confidence. Sometimes students shut down when they feel overwhelmed, so breaking it down helped them to not get those feelings about writing. Through this process, we slowed down each step and worked over several days. Because students didn't feel rushed they were able to dig deeper into each texts and develop strong arguments. I noticed that their confidence grew over each day and they were more engaged when it came time to write their essays.

Think back to a moment during the lesson sequence that stands out. It could be a comment from a student, an unexpected challenge or success, a shift in engagement, or a moment when your own thinking changed. Describe what happened and why this moment feels meaningful to you now. What does it reveal:
One moment that stood out to me was after we spent time analyzing articles and watching videos about fast fashion; students began generating thoughtful, meaningful questions about the topic. They wanted to know what would be the benefits of fast fashion or how anyone could argue that fast fashion was good. So we took it a step further and explored ways that fast fashion might be beneficial. Many students were surprised by this shift, as they had only been considering the negative impacts. At first, they struggled to identify any positives, which revealed how strongly they had internalized a one-sided perspective. However, as we discussed ideas like affordability, accessibility, and the ability for people to express themselves through trends, students began to see that the issue is more complex than they initially thought. This pushed them to think more critically and consider multiple perspectives, rather than viewing the topic as simply “good” or “bad.”
