Looking back on this inquiry cycle, what did you learn about your teaching and your students that you might not have noticed otherwise? How did engaging in this teacher-research process shape or shift your thinking about your classroom practice?:

I think I assumed the students I was working with would complain about writing since they always have in the past. However, the way this assignment was set up allowed them to write their thoughts without feeling constrained by a list of formal writing requirements. The students did a great job.

Think back to a moment during the lesson sequence that stands out. It could be a comment from a student, an unexpected challenge or success, a shift in engagement, or a moment when your own thinking changed. Describe what happened and why this moment feels meaningful to you now. What does it reveal:
When the students were talking about their thoughts on school starting times, I was certain students would think school needed to start later. However, I was pleasantly surprised that they actually wished we could go back to our district's old schedule where the middle school started first and ended first. I appreciated how they really thought through their responses and considered sports, homework, time with family, and free time into their opinions.
As you look over the drafts your students produced in this lesson sequence, what early patterns, surprises, or questions are emerging for you? What aspects of your students’ writing are you most curious to explore more deeply with colleagues during our April regroup? What, if any, questions do you h:
As a former ELA teacher turned librarian, I am most curious about why students could so easily write for this assignment but struggle with more formal writing. I am, of course, always interested in grammar, usage, etc, but I am also curious to see how, is using an assignment like this, could possibly lead to more detailed writing.
