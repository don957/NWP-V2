Quicklists

Once students begin to regularly notice arguments in the world around them, they can find even more ideas by looking at their own lives. Every day, we have many experiences and make decisions that relate to larger conversations people are having in the world.

Daily Schedule

One place to discover argument topics is in our daily schedules. Writers can use a clock organizer or create one in their writing notebooks, then chart out how they spend 12 hours in a typical day. They can also simply list their schedule. Students then use their daily schedule to identify possible argument topics. Notice the example below. From this schedule, a teacher generated a topic list in the form of questions:

  • Is breakfast really the most important meal of the day?

  • Best approaches to dog training?

  • Would a block schedule help mitigate learning loss?

  • What are the mental health benefits of taking breaks and going outside?

  • Are there benefits to doing nothing? Is it okay to do nothing?

Quicklists

Another way to find argument topics is by creating a quicklist. We want to give student writers choices, but saying, “write about anything you want” without supporting them is often no choice at all. This strategy gives students a structure for generating a list of potential argument topics that they can return to again and again.

To create a quicklist, have students number from 1 to 10 in their notebooks. Share each prompt (below) and have students quickly jot down their responses. Students return to the list and code it using an A for every topic that lends itself to an argument. These quicklist prompts can be adapted and changed throughout the year to discover more arguments.

Quicklist

  • 1–2 List two things you did yesterday

  • 3–4 Two things you like that others might not

  • 5–6 Two conversations you have had recently (in-person or text/social media)

  • 7–8 Two things you don’t like that others do

  • 9–10 Two things you don’t think you could live without

From here, writers have a ready-made list of argument topics. Students can return to this list for daily short writing, to practice specific argument skills like finding evidence or writing claims, or for longer self-selected research projects.​
