Layering Reading, Writing, and Discussion

🖼️ Framing Our Work

In the fall modules, you explored how writers enter a conversation, first through They Say / I Say, and later by layering deeper thinking through Say-Mean-Matter. In Module 2 of the spring course, you extended this work by using the Atwoodian Table to map the voices and perspectives found across short excerpts on a shared topic. Now, Module 3 takes the next step: moving from excerpts to sets of full-length texts and helping students track how their thinking develops as they encounter new information and perspectives.

📝What to Expect

In this module, you will:

✅ Explore the layering approach as a way to support students in considering a range of perspectives before establishing their own claim

✅ Reflect on the role of talk in literacy routines and how discussion helps students rehearse ideas before writing

🧭 Explore and Learn

After using short excerpts to help students explore and map a conversation, as you did with the Atwoodian Table, students are ready to study a pair or trio of full-length texts (including videos, infographics, and articles).

Through a Routine Writing approach called “layering,” students read through a set of texts and add to their thinking and writing after each new text. As they learn more about a topic and research varying perspectives, their opinions often shift and evolve. This concept invites students to consider multiple perspectives before they make their own claim, and prepares them to manage more than one source in their written arguments.

​In his book Teaching Argument Writing, George Hillocks asserts that a written argument should not begin with the writing of a claim:

The most compelling claims and arguments grow from an exploration and understanding of the multiple perspectives surrounding a topic. Many times, our own thinking will shift as we research and discover new information. We can encourage students to delay making a claim until they have explored an issue more deeply. One way to do this is to guide students through a process of layering reading, writing and discussion as they practice writing and revising their own claims.

As you may have noticed in your work with They Say / I Say, Say-Mean-Matter, and the Atwoodian Table, students often think best by talking first. The same is true in the layering process. Those moments when students turn to a partner, share what surprised them, or compare interpretations are not pauses before the “real” work, they are part of the work. A part of the layering reading, writing, and discussion that I find especially powerful is when discussion allows students to share and compare their ideas with others. This step is powerful because students often hear perspectives they had not considered before, which can help to deepen their comprehension of the text and give them a good debate to engage in. Layering reading, writing, and discussion supports students in developing informed claims by giving them multiple opportunities to engage deeply with a text before they write. Students first interact with a text to gather evidence, after reading, students draft their thoughts in writing. Then discussion starts with students sharing ideas with peers from comprehension to analysis to argumentation in a structured way. All of these steps work together to make their claims well-supported, thoughtful, and credible. One challenge I can imagine in an 8th grade classroom where I teach is ensuring that all students participate meaningfully in the discussion portion. Some students may be hesitant to share their ideas, while others may dominate the conversation. If students don't fully engage in discussion, the layering process loses some of its power, because hearing multiple perspectives is what helps learners refine and strengthen their claims.
