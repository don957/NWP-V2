Do We Need Zoos?

DAY 1

Welcome to the Do We Need Zoos? text set for the Making Claims resource. Try this resource out with your students to support them as they practice writing recursive claims. The resource layers annotated reading, reflective writing, and critical thinking to guide students as they gather information from texts, consider multiple angles on a topic, develop and revise a claim, and draft an 11-minute argument.

Image by BNW from Unsplash​

Full Text Set

The texts listed below will be introduced within the daily lessons. They are provided here to share with students, but you may want to wait and introduce them one at a time as you proceed through the lesson sequence.

Text #1: Photograph that serves as an introduction to the topic. “Family at the Zoo" (Unsplash.com)

Text #2: Infographic detailing interesting facts and information about animals in captivity. “Empty the Tanks” (Animal Welfare Institute).

Text #3: Video that provides context, facts, and information about why zoos are needed. “Why We Need Zoos” (Dallas Zoo, narrated by their Senior Director of Conservation)

Text #4: Article arguing for animal rights. Chan, Melissa. “Animal Rights” (Time for Kids, 2021).

​

Day 1 Lesson Sequence

QUICKWRITE

First, lead students through the quickwrite activity slides below. This activity gets students thinking and talking about the topic. Through this writing experience, students will acknowledge that we come to an idea, topic, or issue with a gut claim, feeling, or belief. It’s important for teachers to not share their own opinions on the topic so students may develop their own ability to make informed claims.

READ TEXT #1

Next, guide students as they read and respond to the photograph (Text #1). Students should use the “Reading” (left) side of their notebook to capture their thinking in response to the following prompts:

  • What is happening in this image? Jot down what you see.

  • Would you feed animals at the zoo? Why or why not?

  • As you look at the photo, what questions do you have about zoos?

DISCUSS

Lead the students in a discussion about the topic. What are they thinking? As students share thinking in small groups or as a whole class, teachers should chart what students see, what students think, and any student questions.

ADD TO WRITING

Ask students to use these sentence frames or others they might think of to add to their writing:

  • The photograph makes me question...

  • Now I am thinking...

  • I’m wondering...

  • Just as I was thinking earlier...

Students add to their initial writing about the topic with new information from the photograph. Guide students to include specific details from the picture, discussion, or charts as they add to their writing.

PROCEED TO DAY 2

DAY 2

The second day of the lesson introduces students to two additional texts with opportunities to read, annotate, and write.

Day 2 Lesson Sequence

WRITING INTO THE DAY

Ask students to re-read their final written entry from the day before. Ask them to add any thoughts or questions to their thinking from yesterday.

READ & ANNOTATE TEXT #2

Provide students with Text #2, "Empty the Tanks", an infographic published by Animal Welfare Institute fighting to free Orcas in captivity. Ask students to use the following annotation strategies while reading the text.

  • Students should place a ✭ (star) next to key insights they notice in the infographic.

  • Students should use an “F” for each fact they notice that seems important or compelling.

RESPOND

Students should respond to this question: Based on the infographic, what are you thinking now about the need for zoos?

DISCUSS

Next, share thinking in classroom discussions. Teachers continue making classroom charts that capture the students’ thinking about each question below. Refer back to yesterday’s discussion chart to see if any student-generated questions were answered today:

  • What information do you know now that you didn’t know before?

  • What fact was most surprising? Why did it surprise you?

  • Did any of yesterday’s questions get answered? What do you still not know that you’d like to know?

ADD TO WRITING

After discussion, students add to their initial writing about the need for zoos with new information from the infographic. Guide students to include references to specific insights and facts from the infographic as they add to their writing:

  • I didn't know...

  • This _________ makes me wonder…

  • Further supporting my thinking is…

  • Something I never considered is…

​

READ TEXT #3

Explain to students that they are going to layer their learning about the need for zoos by viewing another text and writing commentary, which is one way to respond to a text. Text #3 is a video that provides context, facts, and information about why zoos are needed, “Why We Need Zoos” (Dallas Zoo, narrated by their Senior Director of Conservation).

As you view the video, record on the “Reading” (left) side of the notebook, students record what they see and hear – images, words, colors, information, etc. It might be helpful to watch the video once as a class and instruct students to listen for interesting information. During a second viewing, students can jot down facts and details. This is an effective method for modeling the benefit of rereading a text.

DISCUSS AND CHART

Facilitate a whole-class discussion where students share what they learned from the text. During this time, students may add information to their notebooks.

ADD TO WRITING

Students add to their writing about the need for zoos with new information. Guide students to include references to specific information from the video as they add to their writing. Students might use one of these sentence frames:

  • I understand... However, I still...

  • In some ways I still believe... but...

  • Although the video states...

  • I used to think... but now I am thinking…

PROCEED TO DAY 3

DAY 3

In the third day of the lesson, students read and annotate Text #4 and add to their writing on the topic.

Day 3 Lesson Sequence

WRITING INTO THE DAY

Ask students to review their writer’s notebook from Day 2. Next, invite students to pair-share what they discovered from reading the first three texts, using their notebooks to guide their sharing. Ask students to write a summary of what their partner shared.

​

READ & ANNOTATE TEXT #4

Provide students with Text #4, “Animal Rights” (Time for Kids, 2021). This full article argues for animal rights.

As you facilitate the reading, guide students to code the text by highlighting facts and details that stand out to them. Ask students to use the following annotation strategies while reading the text.

  • Students should place a ✭ (star) next to key insights they notice in the article.

  • Students should use an “F” for each fact they notice that seems important or compelling.

​

WRITE/ANNOTATE

Next, ask students to choose 2-3 annotations from Text #4 to write about in their notebooks. Their responses should be added on the "Reading" (left) side. Invite students to use the sentence frames below to explain their choices.

The quote makes me question...

Now I am thinking...

I’m wondering...

Just as I was thinking earlier...

Students add to their initial writing about the topic with new information from the text. Guide students to include specific details from the text as they add to their writing.

PROCEED TO DAY 4

DAY 4

Day 4 Lesson Sequence

WRITE

Ask students to create an It Says/I Say T-chart in their notebook, like this:

As you facilitate a whole-class discussion on what students have learned about the need for zoos, draw the chart out on the board and demonstrate how to fill in each side of the chart. It is important that students see how you choose texts for the "it Says" column, and hear your thinking in response to that text in the "I Say" column. Ask students to copy 2-3 of their underlined or coded passages from Texts 1-4 on the left (“It Says”) side of the chart. For each passage added on the left, students should respond to that passage on the right (“I Say”) side of the chart.

WRITING & REVISING CLAIMS

INITIAL CLAIM

Ask students to look through the chart and writing they have created on the topic and write an initial claim about the need for zoos. Encourage them to think beyond pro/con or yes/no. Provide students a list of hedging or qualifying words on the board that are often used in academic writing to strengthen claims, such as: probably, usually, should, most likely, and often. Once students decide their position or claim, they should write it in their writer’s notebook label this claim in the notebook as their “initial claim.” The slideshow above can be used to help teach students what a claim is and how to write their initial claim.

​

SHARE

Ask for several students to read their claims and share one piece of evidence from the texts that helped them to make that claim.

​

PROCEED TO DAY 5

DAY 5

REVISING THE CLAIM

Invite students to review their initial claim and remind them they can revise the claims they have written. Discuss why one might choose to revise their initial claim.

WRITING AN ARGUMENT

Using the process below, guide students through an 11-minute argument draft (adapted from Gretchen Bernabei). Students will write this on the “Writing” side of their notebook. Consider modeling your own draft on a digital whiteboard or chart paper as students write in their notebooks.

  • For 1 minute: Write down your gut reaction about the need for zoos. This is how you felt when we first started talking about zoos.

  • For 4 minutes: Write about what things you should consider when thinking about the need for zoos. Think about what we have learned and what you already wrote about in your writer’s notebook.

  • For 4 minutes: Write about your own position. What is your claim about the need for zoos?

  • For 2 minutes: Write, “Now I am thinking...” and share what you are now thinking about the need for zoos. Or, write, “Maybe we should...” and share what you think we should do about zoos.

  • Share! Read your argument to a partner.
