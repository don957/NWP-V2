Fast Fashion Text Set

Day 1

Welcome to the Environmental Impact of Fast Fashion text set for the Connecting Evidence to Claims Elementary lesson sequence! Try this resource out with your students to teach them to use evidence from source material in order to support their claim. The resource layers annotated reading, reflective writing, and critical thinking to support students as they gather information from texts, consider multiple angles on a topic, curate and quote source material, and finish a draft.

Lesson Sequence

QUICKWRITE

First, lead students through the quickwrite activity slides below. This activity gets students thinking and talking about the topic. They write to acknowledge that we come to an idea, topic, or issue with a gut claim, feeling, or belief. The loop writing strategies will help students build depth into their thoughts on the topic. It’s important for teachers to not share their own opinions on the topic so students may develop their own ability to make informed claims.

SHARE

Distribute students in groups of 2-3. Each student should choose at least one entry from their quickwrite above and share this entry with their group. If time allows, the teacher can invite a few volunteers to share with the entire class.

VIEW AND ANNOTATE TEXT #1

Show students Text #1, a video overview from Our Changing Climate. Although a video cannot be annotated in the traditional way, ask students to listen carefully and jot down key information and details while viewing the video.

ADD TO WRITING

Ask students to add to their writing about the topic with new information from the text. Guide students to include references to specific insights and facts from the text as they add to their writing.

Day 2

The second day of the lesson introduces students to a text that explains what fast fashion is, how it came about, and its impact on the world. Students will have the opportunity to read and annotate the text, then write more about the topic.

Lesson Sequence

Read And Annotate Text #2

Provide students with Text #2, “What is fast fashion? How it's destroying the environment” from Good Housekeeping. Ask students to annotate while reading the text.

Suggestions for annotation:

  • As you read, look for the main claim of the text. Underline and label it “main claim” when you see it.

  • Highlight or place [brackets] around information that seems important.

  • Circle keywords in the text that are important to help you understand the topic.

  • Write the keyword (s) in the margin nearby to enable quick scanning and retrieval of information during the planning and writing process.

Teacher Tip: Consider breaking the text up by sub-headings for a more manageable approach to students learning more about the topic.

Write

Next, ask students to write in their notebooks for about three minutes in response to two or three of the annotations they made while reading the text above.

Share

Ask students to rejoin their groups of 2-3 from Day 1. Each student should share their entry with their group. If time allows, the teacher can invite a few volunteers to share with the entire class.

Day 5

On the fifth day of the lesson, students begin organizing their thoughts on the issue and compose an organized draft on the topic. NOTE: The work of day five may extend over multiple days.

Lesson Sequence

Draw a Graphic Representation

Students should take out the printed texts from previous days and their notebooks. Follow the sequence below:

  • Students draw a simple graphic that represents the relationship between the sources. What position does each source hold in relation to the other sources

  • Students write a short explanation of their graphic.

  • Students share their graphics and writing with a partner, making any updates to their graphics based on their partner conversations.

  • Lastly, students add themselves to the graphic. Where do they stand on the issue in relation to the sources? Students should draw themselves in the graphic, adding any necessary annotation to clarify their position, and marking their position in the conversation.

Write an Initial Claim

Ask students to return to their notebooks and reread everything they have written on the topic, including their quickwrite and loop writing, their graphic, and any writing they produced in response to the annotation of each text. Ask students to write a 1-2 sentence claim taking a position on the issue. Some questions to help them in this process include:

  • Has your thinking changed after reading these texts?

  • What text(s) most informed your thinking?

  • What influenced your thinking the most in the process of becoming informed?

Ask students to share out their claims in groups of 2-3, naming their initial claim and listing key information that is currently influencing their claim. Students can revise their initial claim to ensure it is debatable and defensible. Remind students they may revise or change their claims as they plan and draft their argument.

Connecting Evidence and Commentary

The slideshow below leads your students through the process of organizing their thoughts into a paragraph where their claims are supported by evidence and developed by commentary.

Write An Argument

The slideshow above can help your students develop argumentative paragraphs that include claims, evidence (with sentence templates), and commentary. There are multiple ways that these parts can be ordered together to form a paragraph. Once they have organized these parts into their first paragraph, students can turn to the next page and repeat the process with more evidence and commentary. In each of these paragraphs, students should look to build on their argument with a sequence of claims, each supported by evidence and explained through commentary.

The completed paragraphs can then be used to structure an organized argument. Drafts may be anywhere from 3-6 paragraphs, depending on the amount of evidence they compile to support their main argument.
