Endangered Species and Green Energy Projects

Day 1

Welcome to the Endangered Species and Green Energy Projects text set for the Ranking Evidence resource! Try this resource out with your students to teach them to evaluate the quality and relevance of evidence to support a claim. As with all NWP lesson sequences, this resource layers annotated reading, reflective writing, and critical thinking surrounding a topic. Students will:

  • Analyze the use of evidence in sources representing multiple perspectives on an issue

  • Select and rank evidence based on its relevance to the claim

  • Order evidence to plan for logical reasoning

  • Draft an argument

Full Text Set

The texts listed below will be introduced within the daily lessons. They are provided here to share with students, but you may want to wait and introduce them one at a time as you proceed through the lesson sequence.

TWO SHARED TEXTS

Text #1: Radio news segment explaining how decades-old environmental protection laws may be used to stop environmentally friendly projects that are intended to slow climate change. “How green laws stop green projects.” (NPR, 2022). You may also read the transcript.

Text #2: Opinion news article explaining the effects of wind turbines on endangered bats, birds, and insects. “Why wind turbines threaten endangered species with extinction.” (Forbes, 2019).

​

FIVE JIGSAW TEXTS

Text #3: News article explaining the findings of a science research article about the effects of climate on global biodiversity. “One-third of all plant and animal species could be extinct in 50 years, study warns.” (USA Today, 2020).

Text #4: News video about the benefits and challenges related to removing dams that generate carbon-free electricity. “Salmon vs Dams.” (Oregon Public Broadcasting, 2017).

Text #5: Web page from environmental group stating support for wind projects as long as harm is reduced for birds. “Wind power and birds.” (National Audubon Society, 2020).

Text #6: News article about a compromise reached that would involve installing bird and bat detection technology on wind turbines. “Compromise calls for high-tech bird, bat monitoring at Lake Erie wind project.” (Energy News Network, 2019).

Text #7: Solar panel company web blog post about possible harms to birds caused by solar panels and climate change. “Protecting feathered friends: Are solar panels a threat to birds?” (Westbridge Energy Corp., 2021).

Lesson Sequence

QUICKWRITE

On the board, write “Relevant Evidence” and ask students to jot down factors that make a piece of evidence logical and relevant. Ask them to consider what makes them choose a particular piece of evidence or pick one piece of evidence over another. Next, ask students to share while you chart their responses on the board to begin an anchor chart.

EVIDENCE RANKING

The purpose of the Evidence Ranking activity is to begin the conversation about what makes logical, relevant evidence. This activity is highly supported, with the claims and evidence being provided for students so they can practice the process of evaluating evidence related to a claim, the skill they will apply later on when mining texts for evidence.

Pass out copies of the Evidence Ranking Activity Handout (← force copy link) and explain that students will rank the evidence for each claim from most effective to least effective. Depending on student experience with claims and evidence, you may want to model one first or do a shared ranking with the class.

As students work through the handout and evaluate the provided evidence, keep adding to the criteria list on the anchor chart. Keep this anchor chart handy so that students can refer to it throughout the rest of this lesson sequence.

Day 2

The second day of the lesson introduces students to two shared texts that help students become aware of the topic. Students practice annotation with teacher support and write their initial claims about the issue.

Lesson Sequence

​FIRST SHARED TEXT

Show the first shared reading text for this lesson, “How green laws stop green projects.” (NPR, 2022). You may also want to have the transcript available for students. Students can use their notebooks to jot down key information and details while listening to the audio segment.

​SECOND SHARED TEXT

Provide each student with a copy (digital or hard copy) of the second shared text for this lesson, “Why wind turbines threaten endangered species with extinction.” (Forbes, 2019).

Divide the article into three parts for a gradual release modeling of annotation:

Part One: Read the first portion of the article aloud and model annotation for your students while thinking out loud, focusing on the author’s use of evidence. Students should follow along and annotate their own copy. If possible, use a document camera to demonstrate annotation to your students.

Part Two: In the second part of the article, read aloud and model annotating with suggestions from the students. Ask students what they notice about the claims/perspectives and evidence in each paragraph.

Part Three: In the third portion of the article, ask students to continue reading and annotating in partners or small groups. If students seem ready, have them complete the last few paragraphs individually.

SHARE

As time allows, have students share with a partner or have a class discussion: What is the current conversation about this topic? What are people saying about it?

EXIT TICKET

Hand out 3x5" cards or sticky notes. Ask students to write an initial claim (1-2 sentences) taking a position on the issue and include at least one piece of textual evidence to support their claim. Collect these exit tickets for a quick formative assessment check.

DAY 3

In the third day of the lesson, students take part in a jigsaw activity that teaches them how to mine texts for information independently across a range of source perspectives.

Image from Max Pixel

Lesson Sequence

JIGSAW PREPARATION

To prepare for the jigsaw activity in the next lesson, divide the total number of students in the class by the number of articles. Make 4-5 copies of each article. Note: Copying the articles on different colored paper makes the movement between groups run more smoothly.

JIGSAW TEXTS

Text #3: “One-third of all plant and animal species could be extinct in 50 years, study warns.” (USA Today, 2020).

Text #4: “Salmon vs dams.” (Oregon Public Broadcasting, 2017).

Text #5: “Wind power and birds.” (National Audubon Society, 2020).

Text #6: “Compromise calls for high-tech bird, bat monitoring at Lake Erie wind project.” (Energy News Network, 2019).

Text #7: “Protecting feathered friends: Are solar panels a threat to birds?” (Westbridge Energy Corp., 2021).

Give each student one of the jigsaw texts above. The texts can be distributed randomly to students, they can choose an article, or you can match the articles to specific students based on ability. This is one place where color-coded copies come in handy. Note that the articles vary in length and text complexity to allow for differentiation; there are less complex options for ELL students, students with disabilities or struggling readers, and more complex options for advanced students.

READ AND ANNOTATE JIGSAW TEXTS

Students begin by independently reading and annotating their assigned text, identifying claims made in the text and evaluating the author’s use of evidence.

​SHARE

Direct students to meet in small groups with other students who read the same article. Students discuss their annotations and practice explaining their article to the other students.

After they meet in small groups, pass out the Jigsaw Notecatcher (← force copy link) to each student. Help students reorganize themselves into groups of 3-4, all with different articles. Students take turns explaining his or her article while the other group members take notes. Encourage conversation about the evidence as students work together to complete their notecatchers.

As time allows, close the lesson with a class discussion of what students are currently thinking about the topic based on the articles they read.

DAY 4

In the fourth day of the lesson, students make a plan for their draft by evaluating the available evidence from the source material.

Lesson Sequence

Begin the lesson by having each student get out their notecatcher and the texts they have annotated, including the shared texts. Students individually review their notecatcher and the texts, then select 2-4 of the most logical, relevant pieces of evidence they will use to support their working claim about the topic. For this activity, students will also need a copy of this planning sheet (← force copy link) referenced in the slideshow below.

PROCEED TO DAY 5

DAY 5

In the fifth day of the lesson, students draft their argument by following the plan they made in the previous day.

Lesson Sequence

DRAFT AN ARGUMENT

Students begin drafting their argument, following the plan they developed on the planning sheet. Make sure students have access to their annotated texts, notecatchers, and planning sheets. You may want to incorporate some peer review to assist students with their drafts.

REFLECT

After students have completed a draft, ask them to reflect on their process and write a self-assessment for a final step. Depending on time and your students’ experience with this kind of reflection, this could be done independently by the student or during a student-teacher writing conference. Ask students to read back through their arguments and respond to the prompts below:

  • How did you select the evidence you used in your argument?

  • Why did you organize your evidence in the order you did?

  • How would you rate your overall use of evidence? Did you select logical and relevant evidence to support your claim?

  • What would you do differently if you had the opportunity to write the argument again?

​OPTIONAL REVISION

For a revision opportunity, have students use what they discovered with their self-reflection to write another draft of their argument.
